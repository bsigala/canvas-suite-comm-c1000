Discussion Etiquette & Commenting Models

COMM C1000 — Strong Guidelines for Constructive Async Dialogue

Threaded Discussions & Community

Discussions in COMM C1000 are forms of asynchronous communication. This means we are not meeting live, but rather posting questions, arguments, outlines, and video check-ins to a shared discussion board where your peers can read and reply to them at a later time.

Discussions are accessible to all participants in our Canvas workspace, so they are not the place for private inquiries. (For personal questions, please always use the Canvas Inbox). Threaded formatting helps keep our dialogue highly organized and contextualized, making it easier for everyone to follow along!

The "3 C's and a Q" Response Model

When replying to your classmates' discussion posts, your responses should deepen the conversation and offer substantial insights, not merely agree with what they wrote. To achieve this, you are expected to use the **3 C's and a Q** commenting model:

3 C's and a Q Purpose & Guidelines Sentence Starters & Examples
Compliment Start positive. Validate your classmate's perspective, or offer a compliment about a specific, compelling point they made.
  • I really liked your point about...
  • I understand how you came to the conclusion that...
Comment Add substantial thoughts to what was said. Express agreement or respectful disagreement, backed up by your observations or readings.
  • I agree with your stance because, for example, ...
  • I respect your perspective, but I came to a different conclusion because...
Connect Build a personal connection. Relate their post to your own experience, something you have read in our textbook, or observed elsewhere.
  • The same thing happened to me when...
  • I read a similar article in our OER text which notes...
Question Keep the conversation alive! Pose a constructive follow-up question that prompts your classmate to expand on their ideas.
  • I wonder what effect X might have on...
  • How might you feel differently about this topic if...

Succeeding on Discussion Boards

1. Formulate Open-Ended Insights

A good discussion post avoids simple yes/no answers. If a prompt can be answered in one word or is a factual recall from our readings, it makes for a poor discussion. Focus on sharing your unique arguments and personal connections to the topics!

2. Narrow Your Scope, Ground with Personal Context

Avoid being overly broad. Instead of giving a generic overview of an article, describe how the article relates to a specific personal event or speech topic you are interested in. This makes your initial posts far more engaging for your peers to review!

3. Space Your Contributions Out

Always submit your posts throughout the designated discussion window rather than submitting them all on the final day. Spacing your replies out gives your peers plenty of time to read, reflect, and keep our class-wide dialogue active.
